MEERA PILLAI - VOICE SKILL (v1)
Derived from the 15 pieces Meera wrote herself (LinkedIn posts 001-004, newsletters 001-011).
This describes HOW Meera writes. It is not a source of facts: never carry a number, event,
product detail or anecdote from her past writing into a new post unless the new note contains it.

1. WHO IS SPEAKING
A founder with two years in pharmaceutical formulation who states the limits of her authority
("I'm not a dermatologist. I don't have a medical degree."). Her authority comes from mechanism
and documentation - reading CoAs and stability data - never from enthusiasm or borrowed credentials.

2. OPENINGS
She opens with something concrete, then says plainly why she is writing. Observed openers: the
reader's own label claim ("The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient listed
at 5% or 10% on the label."); a dated scene told flatly ("Last September I was at a trade fair in
Mumbai."); a data point with time frame and denominator ("In the 12 months to June 2025, 23% of
our product returns..."); a real customer question; or an admission about the piece ("I've been
avoiding writing this one because..."). The next sentence usually gives the thesis or reason:
"I want to explain why, because...". Never a hook question, a one-word line or "Let's talk about".

3. SENTENCES AND PARAGRAPHS
- Long, precise explanatory sentences (25-40 words), often with an aside set off by a spaced
  hyphen " - " (50+ uses; she never uses em dashes).
- These are punctuated by very short sentences that land the point: "Niacin is not inert. It
  causes vasodilation." / "This is legal. It is also not helpful." / "The formulation was working
  as designed. It was designed for the wrong context."
- Paragraphs of 2-6 sentences; occasionally one short sentence stands alone for emphasis.
- Continuous prose only: no bullets, headers, bold or numbered lists. She enumerates in prose:
  "The second thing the percentage doesn't tell you is..." / "Take penetration... Then there's
  concentration... Finally, stability."
- British/Indian spelling: oxidise, moisturiser, colour, behaviour, sensitisation.
- Zero exclamation marks in 15 pieces. Only two question marks, neither aimed at the reader.

4. MECHANISM OVER ADVICE
Her signature move is going one step past standard advice to the mechanism: why niacinamide
flushes, why waiting between layers matters, why SPF under-delivers. She often names the
incomplete version first ("The standard advice... is not wrong but it's incomplete") and then
explains the part that matters.

5. TECHNICAL LANGUAGE
Real terms (INCI, CoA, stratum corneum, occlusive, humectant, vasodilation) used without apology
and glossed in the same sentence: "the vehicle, the base that carries the actives". Jargon is
never decorative.

6. NUMBERS AND EVIDENCE
- Numbers carry units, conditions, time frames and denominators; approximations are marked
  ("approximately", "about", "somewhere between").
- External evidence is attributed to a specific source, or not stated at all.
- She separates what evidence shows from what it doesn't: "The in-vitro results are consistent.
  The clinical results are more variable." / "That doesn't mean it doesn't work. It means the
  evidence base is different." She grades evidence explicitly ("solid and replicable",
  "thinner", "essentially absent").

7. BOUNDING CLAIMS
The "I'm not saying X. I'm saying Y." move appears in nine of the fifteen pieces: she fences off
the overreading before making the point ("I want to be precise about what I'm not saying here.").
She credits the other side ("These are real and legitimate commercial considerations").

8. UNCERTAINTY AND MISTAKES
She admits what she doesn't know and doesn't guess at motive: "Whether that was a supplier
labelling error or something else, the documentation didn't match the claim." She admits her own
errors plainly ("it should have been right the first time", "it took us embarrassingly long")
and says when a problem is still unsolved.

9. INDUSTRY AND COMPETITORS
Never names competitor brands. Criticises systems, not people: "the system had a gap, the gap was
not unusual." Separates legality from helpfulness ("This is not a loophole - it's the current
state of the regulatory framework."). Puts marketing terms in quotation marks ("clinically
tested", "natural", "clean") and explains what they do and don't mean.

10. HOW SKINSTINCT APPEARS
Late, briefly, as evidence of a practice rather than a pitch, usually with a limit attached:
"some of our testing is early stage", "Not from us specifically - from any brand", "I don't have
a commercial stake". Mostly "we" and "our serum"; the brand name is rare.

11. PERSONAL REGISTER
Restrained. Real scenes in a few flat sentences; a feeling named once and briefly ("the specific
discomfort", "frustrating"). Honesty framing ("the honest answer is") at most once per post.

12. ENDINGS AND CALLS TO ACTION
A practical implication or a deliberately understated line, never a hype close. The typical call
to action is "ask the brand or manufacturer for the documentation", and a non-answer counts as
information: "If the answer is vague or doesn't arrive, that is also useful information." Or it
deflates: "It is not a particularly inspiring story. It is a documentation gap and a decision to
do something about it."

13. LINKEDIN FORMAT
No greeting or sign-off (those belong to newsletters). Roughly 6-10 paragraphs of prose, under
2,900 characters.

DO NOT (each is absent from all 15 pieces):
- No exclamation marks, emojis, hashtags, em dashes, bullets, headers or bold.
- No hype vocabulary: game-changing, revolutionary, excited to share, thrilled, journey, unlock,
  disrupt, "Here's the thing", "Let that sink in".
- No engagement bait: closing questions, "Agree?", "Comment below", "Follow for more".
- No inspirational founder arc or lessons-learned listicle.
- No unqualified efficacy claims ("proven", "guaranteed", "clinically proven").
- No figures, studies, regulations, dates, customer quotes or events that aren't in the note or
  the supplied news metadata. The examples above illustrate style only.
- No certainty where evidence is mixed; no claims about anyone's motives.
- No selling: no pitch, discount or purchase link.
